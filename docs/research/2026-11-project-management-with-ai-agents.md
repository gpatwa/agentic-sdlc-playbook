# Agents move the project bottleneck to review

**Short answer: yes, there is a new way to do project management, but it is a shift in what you plan around, not a new tool category.** When agents write the code, effort stops being the scarce resource. Review, verification and clear specs become the scarce resources, and planning has to be built around them. The evidence behind this is lopsided. Many people report it, a few vendor datasets measure it, and almost nobody has run a controlled test of the planning methods themselves. No study compares estimation accuracy, sprint predictability or schedule slip for agent-heavy teams and conventional ones. The best measured signal is that output counts rise while review time, PR size and incidents rise too, and company-level delivery metrics barely move. The tools market is converging on one pattern: a human-owned tracker feeds an agent runner, and a human reviews the resulting PR. The approval and audit story in that pattern is thin everywhere except GitHub. For Aveto, this is good news and a warning. Its per-slice approval model (Charter approved by hash, human merge, event log) is stronger than what most trackers document. But it has nothing above the single slice and nothing that models the one resource the evidence says matters most, which is the human reviewer. **Recommendation: do not build a tracker, a roadmap tool or a story-point replacement. Build four small things: a recorded fit check, estimate calibration from the 13 runs already on file, a review-capacity measure (owner touches and reviews waiting), and a read-only ticket-to-intent adapter, in that order.**

Labels used below: **[Measured]** means a controlled or telemetry result. **[Reported]** means a company or executive says so. **[Opinion]** means a practitioner argument with no data. **[Secondhand]** means I could only reach a summary of the source.

## Planning, estimation, tickets and status all change at the same joint

**Estimation.** The common argument is that coding was the expensive part of a story, so points and velocity measured effort that has now collapsed. **[Opinion]** One Scrum.org author proposes replacing velocity with an "Agent Efficiency Score" and treating token budgets as the new capacity planning, but the posts cite no data and the formula's penalty term is undefined ([Scrum.org](https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/velocity-agent-efficiency-evidence-based-management-ai-era), [Scrum.org](https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/how-do-sprint-planning-when-half-your-team-are-ai-agents)). The one consistent idea across vendor and practitioner posts is to cap agent work at review capacity, not at generation capacity ([Augment Code](https://www.augmentcode.com/guides/engineering-capacity-planning-with-ai-agents)). That is plausible but untested. **[Measured]** The only hard number about estimating is that developers mis-forecast their own AI-assisted speed. In METR's randomized trial, developers predicted a 24% speedup and afterward believed they had gone 20% faster. They were in fact 19% slower ([arXiv 2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)). That trial used early-2025 tools on experienced developers working in their own mature repos, so it does not describe 2026 agents. METR's 2026 follow-up showed speedups, but with intervals that include zero, and METR itself calls the result unreliable. Developers refused to work without AI, withheld tasks, and ran agents in parallel, which broke time tracking ([METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/)). Do not cite "developers are now 18% faster". The practical lesson is that self-reported speedups cannot rebase an estimate, and per-ticket duration is a weak unit when one person supervises several agents.

**Tickets and specs.** **[Reported, vendor docs]** GitHub tells users to treat an issue assigned to Copilot as a prompt: clear problem, acceptance criteria, file pointers, and no assignment of ambiguous, sensitive or broad work ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/tutorials/coding-agent/get-the-best-results)). **[Measured, observational]** Scope matters in the data: a study of 220,612 PRs found well-scoped agent PRs merge more often than context-heavy ones, though this is correlation from popular open-source Python repos ([arXiv 2607.21832](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21832)). Spec-driven tools (Kiro, Spec Kit) add requirements, design and task files. **[Opinion, one hands-on review]** Birgitta Böckeler found them heavyweight for small changes, turning a small bug into four user stories with 16 acceptance criteria, and said she would "rather review code" than the markdown ([martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/exploring-gen-ai/sdd-3-tools.html)). Thoughtworks says there is no systematic way yet to evaluate specs ([Thoughtworks](https://www.thoughtworks.com/en-us/insights/blog/agile-engineering-practices/spec-driven-development-unpacking-2025-new-engineering-practices)). My synthesis, not a stated finding: a ticket is now a prompt plus acceptance criteria, and the "contract" part comes from deterministic checks. Stripe's published design shows this: one-shot agents, lint in under five seconds, at most two CI rounds, every merged agent PR human-reviewed. Stripe says over 1,000 agent PRs merge per week, which is self-reported with no rework data ([Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/minions-stripes-one-shot-end-to-end-coding-agents)).

**Status reporting.** **[Reported]** Linear Agent now drafts project updates and answers "what is at risk" ([Linear](https://linear.app/changelog/2026-03-24-introducing-linear-agent)). **[Opinion, thin]** Practitioner blogs warn that smooth AI summaries can label an amber project green and hide uncertainty. I found no study measuring this, and most of these pages were read only as search summaries ([StoryPointLab](https://www.storypointlab.com/blog/ai-agile/ai-agile-reporting), [JobRise](https://jobrise.io/en/blog/ai-for-project-managers/)). The defensible principle is my inference: derive status from system events (PR, CI, merge, deploy) and keep facts separate from model judgement.

## The measured evidence says output rises and the queue moves downstream

**[Measured, vendor telemetry]** Faros (10,000+ developers, 2025) found high-adoption teams completed 21% more tasks and merged 98% more PRs, but PR review time rose 91%, PR size 154% and bugs per developer 9%, with no significant company-level gain in throughput, DORA metrics or quality ([Faros](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering)). Its 2026 report (about 22,000 developers) repeats the shape with larger numbers: tasks per developer +33.7%, median time in review +441.5%, PRs merged with no review +31.3%, monthly incidents +57.9% ([Faros](https://faros.ai/blog/ai-acceleration-whiplash-takeaways)). Both compare each organisation's low and high adoption periods without randomisation, Faros sells analytics, and the 2025 data predates current agents. **[Measured, survey]** DORA 2025 (about 5,000 respondents) found AI adoption now positively associated with throughput but still negatively with delivery stability, and calls AI an amplifier of existing engineering systems ([Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report)). DORA has since said it will not run a 2026 survey, according to a single secondary report ([LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/?p=36788)), so the last large public series has ended.

**[Measured, observational]** A difference-in-differences study of 806 Cursor-adopting repos against 1,380 matched controls found a velocity jump only in the first two months, with static-analysis warnings (+30%) and complexity (+42%) staying up ([arXiv 2511.04427](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04427)). Merge rates vary hugely by agent and task, from about 43% to 84% in one study of agent PRs ([arXiv 2607.21832](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2607.21832.md)), so no single "agent acceptance rate" is quotable. **[Reported, practitioner testimony]** At a DX conference, Vanguard said coding was 30% faster but end-to-end cycle time was unchanged, because design review (4 days), security review (5 days) and deployment approval (2 days) came after the 2-day agent build ([DX](https://getdx.com/uploads/dx-annual-insights-2026.pdf)). This is a selected, unmethodical anecdote, but it fits the telemetry.

Self-report points the other way and should not be mixed in. Atlassian's 2026 survey (sample size not stated) says 87% of developers feel AI helps them ship faster, while the share saving ten or more hours a week fell from 68% to 25% year on year, and 60% say AI usage is treated as a productivity signal ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/developer/developer-experience-report-2026)). **What is not measured at all:** estimation accuracy, sprint predictability, reviewer minutes per agent PR, change failure rate from non-vendor data, long-run maintenance cost, business outcomes, and any valid randomized result for 2026 agent tools.

## Roles are blurring in claims, with little measurement behind them

**[Reported, secondhand]** The most-cited company is Amazon, and its primary source is unread. I could not fetch the GeekWire articles (blocked) or the revised SDE guidelines themselves, which Amazon reportedly has not published. What follows rests on a rehost and search summaries ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-rewrites-its-software-engineering-playbook-for-an-era-when-ai-writes-the-code/), [rehost](https://bullsource.com/technology/amazon-rewrites-its-software-engineering-playbook-for-an-era-when-ai-writes-the-code/)). The memo is reported to say teams are measured on outcomes, not inputs; that usage metrics should be used "with care and judgement"; and that work not helped by AI is "a finding, not a failing". It also reportedly warns that some engineers and managers have become less connected to customers. Dates conflict across outlets (27 October, about 3 November, and September in one aggregator). Amazon's AI division reportedly runs teams of six to eight, doing work that once took 30 to 40 people, with a "median 4.5x" gain: these are unaudited executive figures ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/how-agentic-ai-is-rewiring-amazons-teams-and-upending-its-traditions/), search summary only). The rehost does not mention PM, EM or TPM titles. Do not claim Amazon changed those roles.

**Other claims, all weak.** **[Reported]** Block cut about 4,000 jobs (about 40%) in February 2026 and Dorsey and Botha argue that middle managers mainly relay information and that a "customer signal" can generate the backlog directly. The authors say parts "will likely break before they work", and a developer-relations manager quoted by LeadDev argues the cause was a bloated workforce, not AI ([LeadDev](https://leaddev.com/ai/blocks-40-ai-driven-layoff-is-a-warning), [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/04/02/jack-dorsey-roelof-botha-ai-middle-management/)). **[Opinion/anecdote]** Meta PMs rebranding as "AI builders" is a single Business Insider item seen through an aggregator. **[Measured, weak]** The Yale Budget Lab analysis found AI had not yet meaningfully shifted the overall job market ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/02/02/ai-labor-market-yale-budget-lab-ai-washing/)). **[Analyst view]** Gartner warns that organisations widening manager spans without accounting for agent complexity will likely need to rehire middle managers ([Gartner](https://www.gartner.com/en/articles/agentic-ai-for-chros)). I found no source at all about TPMs, and no measured study of PM or EM time before and after agents.

**What I will assert, labelled as inference.** PMs and EMs do not disappear in the evidence; their duties move to writing agent-ready specs, setting acceptance checks, protecting review capacity and deciding what not to hand to an agent. Review is the common named constraint across Faros, Scrum.org, Augment and Amazon's reported sign-off rules, so an EM's effective span is bounded by review throughput, not headcount. Treat that as a reasoned reading, not a result. One caution applies to the accountability question: reports that Amazon's Kiro agent caused a 13-hour outage are contested, and Amazon says misconfigured access controls were to blame ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/ai/13-hour-aws-outage-reportedly-caused-by-amazons-own-ai-tools-170930190.html), [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/software/ai/reports-claim-an-aws-outage-last-year-was-caused-by-an-ai-coding-tool-deciding-to-delete-and-recreate-the-environment-from-scratch-while-amazon-says-misconfigured-access-controls-were-to-blame)). Who is accountable when an agent merges is not settled in any public policy text I found.

## The tools keep the human tracker and add agents as assignees

**Linear.** **[Reported, vendor]** Linear made agents assignable in May 2025, with the human staying primary assignee and the agent as a contributor ([Linear](https://linear.app/agents)). It shipped its own Linear Agent in public beta on 24 March 2026 and cloud Coding Sessions (Claude Code, Codex) in June ([Linear changelog](https://linear.app/changelog/2026-06-11-coding-sessions)). Its "issue tracking is dead" essay claims agents are installed in over 75% of enterprise workspaces and wrote nearly 25% of new issues; these are unverified company numbers, and the CEO also said issues "will remain the core items" ([Linear](https://linear.app/next), [The Register](https://theregister.com/2026/03/26/linear_agent)). Coding Sessions defer approval to GitHub review rules and describe no separate approval step or audit log ([Linear docs](https://linear.app/docs/coding-sessions)).

**Atlassian.** **[Reported, vendor]** Agents in Jira let teams assign work items to Rovo and third-party agents, in open beta with credit billing from 3 December 2026. The support page describes a per-user "Publish" step, no named accountable role, and no dedicated audit log, though marketing coverage says agents follow "audit trails and approval flows" ([Atlassian support](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-software-cloud/docs/collaborate-on-work-items-with-ai-agents/), [heise](https://heise.de/-11191778)). The two sources conflict on the beta date and on how strong governance is; I take the support docs as the narrower, more reliable text.

**GitHub.** **[Reported, vendor docs, the most explicit]** Copilot cannot approve or merge a PR; the person who requested it cannot approve it; workflows need a human "Approve and run workflows" click by default; it can push to only one branch; and audit-log events and signed commits are recorded ([GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/coding-agent/risks-and-mitigations)). The gate sits at the PR, not the issue. Since 28 October 2026 the "Copilot in the cloud" experience, GA of Agentic Workflows and an "Agent Merge" feature are listed in the changelog, though I read only one entry's body ([GitHub](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-10-28-work-from-anywhere-with-a-new-connected-copilot-cloud-experience/)).

**Agent-native systems.** **[Reported]** Beads, Backlog.md and Taskmaster store tasks in the repo for agents to read and write, with dependency-aware "ready" queries and MCP access. Their star counts show interest, not use ([Beads](https://github.com/steveyegge/beads), [Backlog.md](https://github.com/MrLesk/Backlog.md)). OpenAI's Symphony polls Linear and starts a Codex run per issue, and calls itself an experimental preview. The "500% more landed PRs" claim comes from OpenAI and is not measured evidence ([Symphony](https://github.com/openai/symphony), [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/04/28/openai-symphony-codex-orchestration-linear/)). Vibe Kanban's company shut down in April 2026, which suggests standalone "kanban for agents" struggled commercially while platforms absorb the function ([Vibe Kanban](https://vibekanban.com/blog/shutdown)). Notion, Asana, ClickUp and monday now sell credit-billed autonomous agents, but none ships a code-delivery loop, and their approval models are described as "checkpoints" or admin limits at best.

**What is genuinely new, my judgement:** a task store that agents read and write, the tracker as a queue that a runner polls, and an agent-session protocol attached to an issue. **What is rebranded:** AI triage suggestions, request intake and draft-an-issue features. Across all of them, approval at the work-item level is mostly deferred to GitHub or to the individual user.

## Aveto has strong per-slice governance and almost nothing above the slice

*Source for this section: local files in the playbook and run repos (paths in backticks), inventoried by a read-only pass on 2026-11-04. The evidence base is one operator and 13 runs, so it is a case study, not a trend.*

**What Aveto already has, built and in use.** A Charter per run, approved by its sha256 in the owner's own words, with six always-human action classes and a default end action of "push branch and open a PR; never merge" (`docs/HUMAN_APPROVAL_RULES.md`, `templates/CHARTER_TEMPLATE.md`). An Engineering Manager role that rejects oversized slices and proposes splits (`agents/engineering-manager.md`). A pre-spawn budget hook with plan, ceiling and hard cap (`execution/hooks/budget-guard.mjs`). Per-run records: `STATE.md`, `trace.json`, a hash-chained event log in newer runs, `END_REVIEW.md`, and a generated `/runs` dashboard that says it is "NOT a control plane" and shows "not recorded" rather than inferring. Measured against the market, this is the thing the trackers do not document: a named-person approval, an explicit list of what only a human may do, and tamper-evident logging.

**Aveto's own estimate data is rare and instructive.** Across 13 runs, the first seven finished under plan (17% to 99% of plan), while the five most recent finished at 125% to 157% of plan, and owner-set hard caps ran about 1.6 to 2 times plan (`site/runs.html`, run `STATE.md` files). Review and gate stages ran 1.5 to 3 times their estimates, and one tech-spec stage ran nearly four times (`runs/polished-chat-experience/END_REVIEW.md`). The overruns were absorbed by wider caps, not better estimates. The calibration file that would close the loop, `docs/data/budget-ratios.md`, was proposed and never created (`docs/SESSION_AND_BUDGET_DESIGN.md`). Caveat: tokens here are peak-context sums, and `runs/ANALYTICS.md` totals the same runs at a different scale (5.79M versus 314k for one run). Any citation must name the unit.

**What Aveto lacks, by evidence of absence.** No roadmap, no prioritisation (the routine-run picker orders by file name), no cross-slice dependency data, no project risk register, no stakeholder digest, no ticket intake, no calendar or deadline field, and no model of the human reviewer's capacity. The recorded mismatches matter more than the absences. The DORA table in `runs/ANALYTICS.md` is empty because `landed` reads false in all 13 `trace.json` files, including merged runs. "Owner touches" is defined as a metric but `analyze.mjs` does not compute it. `runs/INDEX.md` is specified in `RUN_INVENTORY.md` but exists in none of the repos checked. Two slice-size thresholds (200k and 600k) disagree, and two runs far exceeded both. Standing Charters and the picker are built but not proven in a run. The 28 agent roles cover one slice each; the docs explicitly exclude a project-manager role and claim no team-scale proof (`docs/AGENT_ROLES.md`, `docs/PLAYBOOK.md`). The T42 plan (`docs/PRODUCTIVITY_AND_ORG_PLAN.md`) already acknowledges the missing outcome metrics, org model, review capacity and parallel-Charter test, and is explicitly "plan, nothing built".

## Build four small things and decline the rest

The decision rule I used: build only what (a) the evidence says is the real constraint, (b) fits the single-owner Charter model, and (c) can be checked from records Aveto already writes. That rule rules out a tracker or roadmap layer, because Aveto's users do not have one yet and vendors with large teams are already shipping them.

| Order | Build | Why (evidence) | Done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fit check with a recorded outcome**, including "AI did not help here, and why" (T42 item F2) | Ticket-quality evidence (GitHub guidance, scope-versus-merge correlation) and Amazon's reported "finding, not a failing" framing. Cheap, and the Charter already exists to carry it | Every intent ends with a fit line in the plan, stored in `trace.json` |
| 2 | **Estimate calibration** from the 13 runs: plan versus actual per stage kind, then set ceilings from it (`budget-ratios.md`) | The only measured planning datum is that forecasts are wrong; Aveto has the data to fix its own and nobody else has published theirs. State the unit | File exists; next Charter's ceilings cite it |
| 3 | **Review-capacity measure**: compute owner touches, time to first touch, and reviews waiting; fix `landed` so lead time and merge counts populate | Review is the one constraint every dataset names; Aveto defines these metrics and does not compute them. This also repairs the empty DORA table | Dashboard shows touches per slice and reviews waiting; DORA row has numbers |
| 4 | **Read-only ticket adapter**: a GitHub Issues (then Linear) issue becomes a draft `intent.md` for the owner to confirm. No write-back, no agent assignee, no sync | The market pattern is tracker feeds runner; Aveto's gate stays the Charter. Optional adapters are already in T24 | One real issue goes issue to intent to Charter to PR |

**What Aveto should not build.** A story-point or velocity replacement, because no evidence supports any replacement and Aveto's budget in tokens already serves as a cost guardrail. A roadmap or portfolio view, because there is one owner and no team to align. A tracker, because the category's one standalone entrant shut down and every large vendor is absorbing the function. AI-written status digests for stakeholders, because the only reported risk is false confidence and Aveto's strength is that its dashboard shows recorded facts only. Per-person productivity metrics, which T42 already rules out and which Amazon's reported KiroRank shutdown (after people overused the tool to climb it) supports ([GeekWire](https://www.geekwire.com/2026/amazon-rewrites-its-software-engineering-playbook-for-an-era-when-ai-writes-the-code/), search summary). Also hold the parallel-slice claim at "one slice per repo at a time" until the O4 test runs; the hooks are partly worktree-aware, but no document or test says how two Charters coexist.

**Positioning point worth keeping.** Linear defers approval to GitHub, Atlassian describes a per-user Publish step, and GitHub gates at the PR. Aveto can truthfully claim a stricter and more explicit approval model than the trackers publish. It cannot claim team-scale proof, a measured productivity gain, or integration with any ticket system today.

## Source quality to keep in view

Four weaknesses limit this report. First, the Amazon primary text (the guidelines and GeekWire articles) was not read. Everything about Amazon is a rehost or search summary, with conflicting dates, and the 4.5x and 6-engineer figures are unaudited. Second, the strongest delivery evidence is vendor telemetry (Faros, LinearB, DX, Jellyfish) that is correlational, covers only customers, and in Faros's 2025 case predates current agents; direction is carried more reliably than magnitude. Third, several figures came through summaries, not the papers: the Stanford "net +15 to 20%" claim has no paper and is excluded from my conclusions, and LinearB, Jellyfish, GitClear, Uplevel and Cui et al. details were not read in full. Fourth, tool facts are vendor descriptions from a fast-moving market, with unresolved conflicts on Jira's beta date, Linear's tier availability, and whether GitHub's Agent HQ third-party agents are GA. Nothing was found on Shopify, Spotify or Microsoft internal practice beyond 2025 secondhand items, and nothing on TPMs.

## Conclusion

The new way of managing agent-built software is less about new tools than about moving the scarce resource. Generation is cheap, so the plan must be written around reviewable units, clear acceptance checks and a human who can only read so much per day. The surprising result is how little of this has been measured: no one has shown that any replacement for points or velocity forecasts better, and the one randomized trial everyone cites is dated and now disowned by its own authors for 2026 tools. That makes Aveto's own run records unusually valuable. Thirteen runs with plan-versus-actual data, named approvers and tamper-evident logs are small, but they are more than most vendors publish.

For Aveto, the risk is not missing a feature. The risk is spreading thin across PM functions it was never designed for, while the metric most tied to its owner's real constraint, review attention, stays defined but uncomputed. Fix the records it already writes, add the fit check and calibration, then test one real ticket through the Charter. If a second human reviewer ever joins, revisit roles, span and shared gates. Until then, the right project-management product is a small, honest one.
